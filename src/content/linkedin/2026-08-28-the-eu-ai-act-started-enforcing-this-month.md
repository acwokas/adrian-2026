I've been reviewing the EU AI Act enforcement notices for several months now - the ones that went live on August 2 - partly because clients have been asking, and partly because I run commercial operations and governance at an AI company and wanted to see how our own house looks against it.

Transparency obligations are now enforceable. Chatbots must identify themselves as AI. Deepfakes must be labelled. These aren't the high-risk rules everyone's been watching - those got pushed to December 2027. These are the quieter ones that landed while most people were still exhaling about the deadline extension.

I wonder how many SLTs have actually checked which of their tools are affected. Not "we'll get legal to review it" - but practically: listing every customer-facing AI system and confirming it tells the user it's not human. In most large companies, that list is surprisingly hard to assemble. The tools were deployed by different teams at different times, and there's no central register.

Running commercial operations and governance at an AI company gives you a useful vantage point on this. You see the gap between what the technology can do and what the regulation assumes it does. The OECD now tracks over a thousand AI policy initiatives across 69 countries. That's not a single compliance exercise, it's an atmosphere.

I'd suggest the transparency rules are actually the interesting ones. High-risk compliance is expensive but legible - classification, process, deadline. Transparency is harder because it requires you to know what you have.

The question I keep coming back to: could you list every AI system that interacts with your customers, and confirm each one identifies itself? If that takes more than a day, that's the finding.

Worth running that audit before December 2027 turns the conversation from transparency to risk classification.
