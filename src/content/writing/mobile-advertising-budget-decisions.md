Mobile advertising budgets can acquire a strange kind of momentum. Last quarter's allocation becomes this quarter's starting point. A platform reports improving performance. A competitor appears to be everywhere. Spending more begins to feel like the sensible default.

I would start somewhere less exciting: what commercial job are we paying mobile advertising to do?

A budget intended to introduce a new service needs different measures from one intended to capture people already looking for it. Putting both into a single return target can reward the easiest sales while leaving the harder work unexamined.

## Give the budget a specific job

Write down the customer action you want and why it matters commercially. A qualified enquiry, a first purchase and a returning customer are different outcomes. Their value also depends on what happens afterwards.

The [IAB's report on US internet advertising revenue for 2025](https://www.iab.com/insights/internet-advertising-revenue-report-full-year-2025/) provides industry context. Industry expenditure cannot tell an individual business what it should spend. Your economics, customers and operating capacity still determine that decision.

Before approving an increase, establish what the existing budget is producing. Include product margin, fulfilment, discounts and the cost of servicing the customer. Revenue attributed to advertising can look attractive while the underlying contribution remains thin.

Be equally clear about the period over which you expect the investment to pay back. A business with limited cash cannot casually borrow the assumptions of one that can wait much longer.

## Inspect what happens after the tap

An advertising campaign can be competently managed and still send people into an awkward buying experience.

Review the journey on an ordinary phone. Can someone understand the offer quickly? Are the delivery terms visible? Does a form ask for information the business could collect later? Does the checkout make someone create an account before they know the full cost?

I would examine these questions before assuming that more traffic is the answer.

Consider a hypothetical specialist retailer whose acquisition costs have risen. A review finds that its delivery charge appears only near the end of checkout. The retailer first tests clearer delivery information, then compares completed orders and contribution before deciding whether additional advertising is justified.

That sequence creates a better basis for spending.

## Make increases answerable to evidence

Separate the question "did the platform record a sale?" from "did this spending create an additional sale?"

Useful checks can include controlled budget changes, comparisons across appropriate customer groups and a review of how platform results reconcile with actual orders. Document other changes that could affect the comparison.

Agree the decision rule before the test. What would justify increasing the budget? What would trigger a pause? Which costs must remain within bounds?

Then change enough to learn something, while keeping the exposure manageable.
