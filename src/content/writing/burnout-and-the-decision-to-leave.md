Leaving a job can be a difficult decision, particularly when the work once mattered to you. There may be colleagues you care about, financial commitments and a professional identity tied to being dependable.

When work becomes persistently draining, those connections can make it harder to assess what is happening. Another busy month can pass while you wait for a quieter period that never seems to arrive.

The decision deserves time, support and an honest look at the conditions around you.

## Understand the concern and seek appropriate support

The [World Health Organization describes burnout](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon) as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It identifies exhaustion, increased mental distance or cynicism towards work, and reduced professional efficacy. WHO does not classify it as a medical condition.

That description should not become a tool for diagnosing yourself or someone else. Seek appropriate healthcare advice about concerns affecting your health. A qualified professional can assess your individual circumstances and discuss support.

For the workplace conversation, I would begin recording the practical pattern: what work is expected, how long it takes and where recovery time keeps disappearing. This is a record of the job's demands rather than a diagnostic checklist.

## Ask what would need to change

I would question an arrangement that asks an individual to cope indefinitely with work the organisation has failed to resource.

A hypothetical manager might have responsibility for two vacant roles alongside their own. Better diary management may help at the edges. The organisation still needs to decide which work will stop, who will take responsibility and when staffing will change.

Make requests concrete where it is possible to do so. Discuss workload, conflicting priorities, working hours, resources and the behaviour that makes the situation harder.

A promised improvement needs an owner and a date. Otherwise, reassurance can leave the underlying arrangement intact.

Leaders have responsibilities here too. If people repeatedly describe the same pressures, the organisation needs to examine how work is designed and managed.

## Give the decision to stay or leave some structure

There is no universal instruction to resign. Equally, staying indefinitely while waiting for an unspecified improvement is a decision with consequences.

Consider your health, financial position, available support and realistic alternatives. If changes have been agreed, choose a review point and decide what evidence would show they are working.

A hypothetical employee might agree reduced responsibilities for six weeks, then review whether the change actually occurred. That is an example of a workplace arrangement to assess, rather than a suggested treatment or a period in which recovery should happen. Someone in a different position may need a different plan.

You do not have to settle every part of your future at once. The next useful step may be a healthcare appointment, a specific conversation about workload or a careful review of your options.

What matters is creating enough support and space to make the decision with a clearer view of your circumstances.
