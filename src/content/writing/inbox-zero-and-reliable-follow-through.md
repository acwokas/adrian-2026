An empty inbox can feel like a productive finish to the day. The more useful question is whether every commitment still has a reliable home.

Email mixes requests, reference material, decisions, reminders and other people's priorities. Clearing the screen does little for delivery if an important action disappears with the message. The aim should be a system that makes work visible and helps the right things happen.

## Decide what the message means

Processing email involves a series of small decisions. Does this require action? Who owns it? What happens next? When does it matter?

The [Getting Things Done approach](https://gettingthingsdone.com/what-is-gtd/) describes a workflow built around capturing, clarifying, organising, reflecting and engaging. Its relevance here is straightforward: receiving something and deciding what to do with it are separate activities.

Take a hypothetical email asking for comments on a proposal by Thursday. Reading it does not resolve it. A useful next step might be "Review pricing assumptions in proposal", with a deadline and a link to the document.

The message can then be filed because the commitment has moved somewhere dependable. Without that transfer, archiving merely hides unfinished work.

## Keep ownership visible

Email becomes harder to manage when responsibility is implied.

Imagine a customer sends three colleagues a question about a delayed delivery. Each person assumes someone else will respond. The message may remain unread, get flagged by everyone or disappear into several different personal systems.

A practical response establishes one owner, the next action and the expected update. For example: "Alex will confirm the revised delivery date with operations and update the customer tomorrow morning."

That sentence provides more operational value than an elaborate folder structure.

The same principle applies to work you have delegated. If an outcome matters, record what you are waiting for and when you will check it. A sent message proves that a request went out. Delivery requires further evidence.

## Review the commitments beyond email

A reliable system needs a review habit because circumstances change.

Suppose a hypothetical supplier has promised a document by Tuesday. By Wednesday, the relevant email may be several hundred messages down the inbox. A separate list of outstanding commitments makes the missing document easier to spot.

A review can cover upcoming deadlines, unanswered requests, delegated actions and decisions awaiting information. It should also remove commitments that no longer matter. Keeping obsolete work visible creates noise of its own.

The tools can remain simple. A task list, calendar and shared project board may be enough, provided their roles are clear and people use them consistently.

Inbox Zero is a useful working preference when it supports this wider discipline. Some people will prefer to keep a small number of actionable messages in view. The test is whether they can find their commitments and follow through.

I would judge the system by a quieter set of outcomes: fewer forgotten promises, clearer ownership and less time reconstructing what was agreed.
