The easiest part of online learning is finding something to watch. The harder part is making that hour useful when you return to work.

A course can be well produced, interesting and full of sensible advice, yet leave your next decision exactly where it was. I think the answer starts before you press play: be clear about what you want to do differently.

These four habits give learning a better chance of surviving the working week.

## 1. Choose a question, then protect the time

Start with a specific problem you can recognise in your own work.

For example, a hypothetical marketing manager might ask: "How can I explain why our campaign results differ across reporting systems?" That question provides a reason to study measurement. It also helps the manager judge whether a course is relevant.

Then put a modest learning slot in the diary. Thirty minutes you can protect may be more useful than an ambitious programme that disappears whenever a meeting arrives.

Choose one course or resource for that question. Keeping six options open can become another way to postpone the work.

## 2. Close the material and try to remember

Watching someone explain an idea can create a comfortable feeling of understanding. Test that feeling.

After a useful section, close the screen. Write the main idea in your own words, explain it aloud or answer a question without looking at your notes. Then check what you missed.

A [2011 study by Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf) found that retrieval practice improved conceptual learning from the science texts studied. That finding does not guarantee better workplace performance. It does give us a useful reason to include active recall in how we study.

Keep the exercise small enough to repeat. Three questions answered from memory can reveal more than several pages of copied notes.

## 3. Apply one idea

Find a low-risk opportunity to use something you have learned.

A hypothetical team leader studying feedback could prepare one clearer example before their next conversation. They could then reflect on whether it helped the other person understand the issue and agree a next step.

Avoid turning every course into a large transformation project. One useful change is a reasonable return on an afternoon of study. Decide what you will try and what would make the exercise useful before adding another module to your queue.

## 4. Explain what happened

Tell a colleague what you tried, what happened and what remains uncertain. This makes gaps visible and gives someone else a chance to challenge your interpretation.

The certificate may mark completion. Your own test can be more practical: can you explain the idea, use it appropriately and recognise its limits? That is a good place to decide what to learn next.
