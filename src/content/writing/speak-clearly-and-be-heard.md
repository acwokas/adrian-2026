People can struggle to follow a speaker who knows the subject extremely well. Expertise brings detail, qualifications and useful connections. It can also make it difficult to decide what the audience needs first.

My starting point would be to make the listener's job easier.

Whether you are presenting a proposal, contributing to a meeting or addressing a larger room, clarity deserves a few practical choices before and during the conversation.

## Give the audience a clear route

Before preparing slides, write the main point in one sentence. Then decide what the audience needs to understand or do with it.

Build a short route to that outcome. Explain the situation, identify the decision and give the evidence needed to assess your recommendation.

For example, a hypothetical operations leader could open with: "We need to change the delivery timetable because the current promise is creating avoidable service failures." The audience now knows why the discussion matters.

Introduce technical terms when they become useful. If a term requires explanation, provide it without making people feel they should already know.

A clear structure also gives you a route back when a question interrupts your prepared sequence.

## Leave space around the important point

I would mark a few deliberate pauses during preparation: after the central claim, before an important number and after asking a question. Give the audience time to absorb what you have said, and yourself time to choose the next sentence.

In a hypothetical budget presentation, the speaker might explain the proposed investment, pause, then describe the expected benefit and its uncertainty. Delivering all three at speed asks the audience to hold a great deal at once.

Practise aloud. A sentence that reads comfortably can become awkward when spoken. Shorten it until you can say it naturally.

When questions arrive, listen to the whole question. A brief check of what the person is asking can save several minutes of answering something else.

## Make the room work for your voice

Being heard also depends on the setting. Test the microphone, check the remote connection and ask whether people at the back can hear comfortably.

The [US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/Documents/taking-care-of-your-voice.pdf) recommends using a microphone when appropriate, avoiding competition with background noise and resting a hoarse voice. It also advises consulting a doctor about voice problems.

Those are useful reminders to check the conditions before trying to project over a difficult room. Move away from a noisy doorway if you can. Use the available equipment. Build breaks into a long session.

You do not need to fill every silence or deliver every detail you know. Give the room a clear point, enough evidence to consider it and time to respond. That leaves space for a conversation worth having.
