I ran a fireside chat this morning with founders in Malaysia, through Beyond4Tech, and the most expensive sentence in a startup came up inside the first ten minutes. It is four words long. "Our product is for everyone." It sounds like ambition. It behaves like fog.

Most founders think their job is to be wanted by everyone. It is the opposite. Your job is to be unmissable to someone, and a firm but polite no to everyone else. Almost everything good downstream, your pitch, your pricing, your referrals, comes from that one act of choosing. And the choosing is not a flash of insight you have in the shower. You earn it by watching who actually says yes.

The shift comes from subtraction. When I started AIinASIA in 2020, the lazy instinct was to cover AI for everyone. There were already a hundred sites doing exactly that, louder and better funded than me. So I asked who I was genuinely the best person to write for, and the answer was the operator in Asia who has to adopt this stuff, not the tourist who likes to admire it. The market confirmed it, I did not guess it. The pieces written for that one reader travelled. The generic ones sat there. About ten thousand people a month read it now, with no paid distribution, because the right people recognised it was built for them rather than at them.

A founder I mentored had the same lesson in a harder form. Her product could help almost any small business, so she sold to all of them, slowly, building a new feature for nearly every deal. We spent an evening listing her happiest paying customers, and the pattern was unmissable. They were clinic owners who had just hired their first admin person. That trigger, that exact moment in their world, was the whole thing. She rebuilt her go-to-market around it and hit profit fast, because she could finally walk in and say "I know your problem, here are ten people just like you I have already solved it for."

It cuts the other way too, when you are bigger. With the SQREEM and TotallyAwesome integration in 2024 we inherited two businesses and two ideas of the customer, and the temptation in any integration is to keep every account out of politeness. The real work, and we got to it later than we should have, was deciding which customers the combined business was actually built to win, and admitting that some inherited relationships were a perfect customer for somebody else, not for us. Saying that out loud is uncomfortable. It is also the job.

So how do you know you have chosen right? You watch what they do, not what they say. "I'd definitely buy that" in a survey is worth nothing. A signature, a renewal, a referral is worth everything. Speed is the tell. The right fit reaches a result quickly, while slow and painful deals are usually a fit problem wearing a process costume. The strongest signal of all is the referral. If a customer cannot describe you clearly enough to send you someone who looks just like them, your positioning is still blurry to them.

The fear sitting under all of this is market size, so let me name it. A niche is a beachhead, not a prison. Amazon started with books. Narrowing the customer usually widens your reach, because a sharp message travels and a generic one gets ignored. PromptAndGo is the clean version. The global incumbents had more money, more content, more reach, and I could not out-broad them, so I out-specified them, with prompts built for the Asian operator's market, register and language. That is a position a global player cannot copy without becoming a worse fit for everyone they already serve.

Here is the move you can make tonight. List your wins, the five customers who bought fastest, stayed, or referred, or if you are pre-revenue, the five people most desperate for what you are building. Find the pattern, and look hardest at the trigger, what was happening in their world the week they decided. Then write one honest paragraph: "My perfect customer is [who they are] who has just [trigger]. They feel [the pain] and want [the outcome]. I can reach them via [channel], and they are emphatically not [who I exclude]." If it excludes nobody, it is not finished. Finally, name that anti-customer out loud, because it frees the time and budget for the people who actually fit. Treat the paragraph as a hypothesis and revisit it every quarter. Done beats perfect.

I did not pick my customer. I noticed which customers were picking me, and I had the discipline to drop the rest. So stop trying to be wanted by everyone. Go and be unmissable to someone. The rest of your strategy gets easier the moment you choose.
