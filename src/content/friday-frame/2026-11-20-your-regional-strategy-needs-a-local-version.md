A regional strategy is very easy to agree on when nobody has to sell it locally.

The slides look sensible. The opportunity is enormous. Every country gets a flag, a growth target and, if they’re lucky, a budget.

Then someone has to make it work.

[This week’s statement from the Singapore Business Federation](https://ihci.sbf.org.sg/docs/default-source/press-releases/press-release-sbf-supports-asean-bac-singapore-engagements-ahead-of-2027-chairship.pdf), looking ahead to Singapore’s ASEAN-BAC chairship in 2027, talks about turning regional cooperation into practical business outcomes. There’s plenty of opportunity in that. There’s also quite a lot of work hiding in the word “practical”.

This week, I ran an interactive workshop on product-market fit with some keen minds from the National University of Singapore. We explored opportunities to help businesses and households reduce food waste. It’s a useful example of why understanding the problem is only the beginning.

A restaurant, a supermarket and a household might all want to waste less food, but that doesn’t mean they need the same solution. Or that the person who benefits is the person willing to pay for it.

“Everyone could use this” sounds encouraging in a pitch. It still leaves you with quite a few questions about who your first customer actually is.

The same applies when you take a business into another market. The opportunity may look familiar, but you still need to understand who buys, what matters to them and what has to happen for your offer to work.

A customer in one market may buy through a partner. Another may expect a direct relationship. Procurement, pricing, regulation and service expectations can all differ. Translating the sales deck won’t necessarily help with any of them, although at least everyone can now understand why it doesn’t quite fit.

That doesn’t mean every country needs to invent its own business.

You need shared standards, sensible controls and a clear idea of what success looks like. But you also need to distinguish those from habits that became “the global process” because someone at head office wrote them down first.

The useful conversation is about what needs to stay consistent, what can change, and who needs to help make that change work.

Local knowledge matters, but so does collaboration. A sales team might spot an opportunity that needs a different delivery model. Finance may see a way to structure the offer. Product may recognise something worth taking into other markets.

That’s much more useful than asking each country to hit its number and hoping everyone joins the dots.

And it works both ways. Local teams should be able to explain why an adaptation matters and how they’ll judge the result. “Our market is different” deserves a proper hearing, but it isn’t a lifetime exemption from being challenged.

This applies beyond geography, too. A process that works for one industry, customer group or function may need adjusting elsewhere. AI can help you compare information and explore options. You still need people close enough to the work to recognise what matters.

Before rolling out the next regional plan, ask the teams delivering it what they would keep, what they would change and where they’ll need support.

Preferably before you’ve finished the slides.
